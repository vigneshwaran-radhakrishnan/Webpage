When Is a Description Complete?

And how would you ever know? A conversation with the 1935 papers, and what came after.

Pick up a steel spoon and describe it: stainless steel, fifteen centimeters, slightly bent. For most purposes, that is all anyone needs. But somewhere inside it are grains, dislocations, a few microscopic voids, trillions of atoms, and electrons doing whatever electrons do. Your sentence mentions none of it. So is the description complete?

In 1935 this question was put, with unusual sharpness, to the most successful theory in physics. Rather than retell the story, I want to ask the original papers directly and let them answer in their own words, then carry the question down to the matter I work on and out to the world as a whole.

Q1. What would it even mean for a description to be complete?

Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen (EPR) began by separating two questions: is a theory correct, and is it complete? Correctness is settled by comparing with experiment. For completeness they proposed a requirement:

“every element of the physical reality must have a counterpart in the physical theory.”Einstein, Podolsky & Rosen, 1935

How they answered: by turning completeness into a matching problem. List what is real, list what the theory can describe, and check that nothing is left over. That moves all the difficulty into one place: deciding what counts as “real.”

Q2. Then how do you decide what is real?

“If, without in any way disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty (i.e., with probability equal to unity) the value of a physical quantity, then there exists an element of physical reality corresponding to this physical quantity.”Einstein, Podolsky & Rosen, 1935

How they answered: operationally. They did not try to define reality in full; they offered one sufficient sign of it. If you can know a value for sure without touching the thing, the value is not something you created by looking; it was already there.

Q3. So, is quantum mechanics complete?

EPR imagined two particles that interact and then fly apart. Measuring the position of the first lets you predict the position of the second with certainty; measuring the momentum of the first lets you predict the momentum of the second instead. Either way, the second particle is never touched. By their criterion, both its position and momentum must be real, yet quantum mechanics cannot give both definite values at once. To escape, one would have to let the reality of the second particle depend on what is done to the first, far away. Their reply to that option:

“No reasonable definition of reality could be expected to permit this.”Einstein, Podolsky & Rosen, 1935
“We are thus forced to conclude that the quantum-mechanical description of physical reality given by wave functions is not complete.”Einstein, Podolsky & Rosen, 1935

How they answered: with a thought experiment and a dilemma. Either the theory is incomplete, or reality at one place depends on choices made at another. They found the second unacceptable, so they chose the first.

Q4. Did they think a complete theory could exist?

“While we have thus shown that the wave function does not provide a complete description of the physical reality, we left open the question of whether or not such a description exists. We believe, however, that such a theory is possible.”Einstein, Podolsky & Rosen, 1935

How they answered: with belief, not proof. It is worth noticing how honest that last sentence is.

Q5. What did Bohr say back?

Bohr replied the same year, under the very same title. He did not dispute the logic; he disputed the criterion of reality itself.

“…the wording of the above-mentioned criterion of physical reality proposed by Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen contains an ambiguity as regards the meaning of the expression ‘without in any way disturbing a system.’”Bohr, 1935
“Of course there is in a case like that just considered no question of a mechanical disturbance of the system under investigation during the last critical stage of the measuring procedure. But even at this stage there is essentially the question of an influence on the very conditions which define the possible types of predictions regarding the future behavior of the system.”Bohr, 1935

And his verdict:

“…the argumentation of the mentioned authors does not justify their conclusion that quantum-mechanical description is essentially incomplete.”Bohr, 1935

How he answered: by widening what a “description” includes. For Bohr, the experimental arrangement is part of the phenomenon. Choosing to measure position rather than momentum on the first particle does not push the second one, but it changes which questions about it can meaningfully be asked. Completeness, on this view, is not about listing every property of an object in isolation; it is about using every unambiguous way of describing what the measuring setup allows.

I find this the most interesting moment in the exchange. Both sides agree on every prediction. They disagree about what a description is for.

Q6. Could anyone settle it, or was it just philosophy?

For almost thirty years it looked like philosophy. Then, in 1964, John Bell took EPR at their word. He opened his paper by restating their position exactly:

“The paradox of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen was advanced as an argument that quantum mechanics could not be a complete theory but should be supplemented by additional variables.”Bell, 1964
“In this note that idea will be formulated mathematically and shown to be incompatible with the statistical predictions of quantum mechanics.”Bell, 1964

If you complete quantum mechanics with extra variables that fix individual outcomes, and you insist that nothing at one place is affected by what is done far away, then the correlations between distant measurements must obey certain limits. Quantum mechanics predicts those limits can be broken. His conclusion:

“In a theory in which parameters are added to quantum mechanics to determine the results of individual measurements, without changing the statistical predictions, there must be a mechanism whereby the setting of one measuring device can influence the reading of another instrument, however remote. Moreover, the signal involved must propagate instantaneously, so that such a theory could not be Lorentz invariant.”Bell, 1964

How he answered: not by deciding who was right, but by turning the argument into mathematics and finding the place where the two pictures predict different numbers. A debate about the meaning of reality became something a laboratory could check.

Q7. And what did nature say?

Experiments by John Clauser, Alain Aspect, Anton Zeilinger and others, recognized with the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, found that nature breaks Bell’s limits. Einstein’s preferred way of completing quantum mechanics, with local hidden variables, is closed.

But notice what this did not show. It did not prove that quantum mechanics is complete. It proved that one kind of completion is impossible. That is a different, and I think more honest, kind of knowledge: not a certificate that the description is whole, but a map of where the missing piece cannot be.

Q8. Does this question matter outside quantum physics?

It is my daily work. I model how metals break. A metal is neither a set of particles you can track one by one nor a featureless continuum. To predict when it tears, my models describe it with a few extra quantities: how much empty space it contains, and the shape, orientation, and spacing of its voids.

In a sense, these are my hidden variables. I added them because the simpler description, stress and strain alone, was demonstrably incomplete: it could not say when the material would fail. With them, the model predicts cracks that match experiments.

Is it complete? Of course not. Real voids are not perfect ellipsoids, and real grains slip, twin, and argue with their neighbors. The honest answer is that the model is complete enough where it has been tested, and I do not fully know where “enough” ends. Under radiation, at extreme temperatures, in places no laboratory reaches, I cannot check. That is exactly where an incomplete description is most dangerous.

EPR asked whether every element of reality has a counterpart in the theory. An engineer is forced to ask a humbler question: which elements does the theory need, for this purpose? Bohr’s point returns here in a quiet form. What counts as a complete description depends on what questions you intend to ask of it.

Q9. Could a description of the whole world ever be complete?

Laplace imagined an intellect that knew every force in nature and the position of every particle; for it, he wrote, nothing would be uncertain, and the future, like the past, would be present before its eyes. That is the dream of a complete description of the world.

Borges answered it, in effect, in a story a single paragraph long: cartographers who made a map of an empire at the scale of the empire itself. It was perfectly complete and perfectly useless, and later generations left it to fall apart in the desert.

Between the two stories sits the real situation. A complete description may be impossible, and if it were possible, it would be no more useful than the world itself. Every useful description leaves something out. That is not a flaw; it is what a description is.

Q10. So how would you ever know?

If completeness is out of reach, the better question is: how would you notice that a description is incomplete? The history above suggests three signals.

  • Surprise. A complete-enough description should not be surprised inside its own domain. Newton’s gravity described the planets almost perfectly, but Mercury’s orbit drifted by a tiny amount it could not explain. That small surprise was the crack general relativity grew from.
  • Intervention. Push the system somewhere new: harder, hotter, faster, smaller. Descriptions that only fit the data they were built on tend to fail the moment you step outside it.
  • Impossibility. Sometimes, as with Bell, you can prove that no description of a certain form can work. You do not learn what is missing, but you learn where not to look.

None of these tells you that you have reached the end. They only tell you, sometimes, that you have not. Maybe that is the most we get: never a certificate of completeness, only better ways of finding the gaps.

I find that oddly reassuring. It means there is always something left to understand, in a spoon, a particle, a piece of metal, or the world.
