One question, one paragraph, drawing on the original papers.

November 16, 2026

What would make a theoretical description of reality complete?

In 1935, Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen gave completeness a precise meaning, though a careful one: “Whatever the meaning assigned to the term complete, the following requirement for a complete theory seems to be a necessary one: every element of the physical reality must have a counterpart in the physical theory. We shall call this the condition of completeness.” Physical reality, in other words, has many elements, and a theory can be complete only if each one of them has a counterpart in it. That turns completeness into a matching problem, so everything hinges on deciding what is real, and here they refused to philosophize: “The elements of the physical reality cannot be determined by a priori philosophical considerations, but must be found by an appeal to results of experiments and measurements.” For that they offered one sufficient sign: “If, without in any way disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty (i.e., with probability equal to unity) the value of a physical quantity, then there exists an element of physical reality corresponding to this physical quantity.” It is a modest statement. They did not try to define reality; they only said how to recognize a piece of it when you see one. Yet those few sentences were enough to put the most successful theory in physics, quantum mechanics, on trial.

Example. Pull a steel bar a little: from the measured displacement, Hooke’s law predicts the force, and if the load cell agrees with the prediction, the force is an element of reality by the EPR criterion. In this range, Hooke’s law is complete, even though it says nothing about, for example, the countless atoms inside the bar; their collective effect is carried by a single number, the stiffness, and that is enough. Pull further, however, and the load cell disagrees with Hooke’s law, because of inelasticity, an element of reality that has no counterpart in the law. A theory, then, can be complete within its own limit of applicability, and only there.

Source: A. Einstein, B. Podolsky, N. Rosen, “Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?” Physical Review 47, 777 (1935). doi:10.1103/PhysRev.47.777
